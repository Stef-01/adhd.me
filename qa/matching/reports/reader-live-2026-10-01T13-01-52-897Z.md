| C7 | explain what ADHD actually is, properly | ADHD (Attention‑Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder) is a neurodevelopmental condition that affects how a person’s brain regulates attention, activity level, impulsivity and executive functions (planning, organising, starting and finishing tasks, managing time and working memory). It has biological roots — differences in brain networks and neurotransmitter systems (especially dopamine and norepinephrine) — and commonly begins in childhood, though it often continues into adulthood and can be diagnosed at any age. Core features: 1) Inattention — difficulty sustaining attention, becoming easily distracted, frequent mistakes from not finishing details, trouble organising tasks, losing things, forgetting appointments or instructions, and struggling to follow through on longer projects. 2) Hyperactivity — appearing restless, fidgeting, being unable to sit still or feeling internally restless (more common in children). 3) Impulsivity — acting without thinking, interrupting others, difficulty waiting, making quick decisions that can have negative consequences. Presentation varies: some people are predominantly inattentive, some predominantly hyperactive/impulsive, and some have a combined presentation. Many people also have related problems with executive function (planning, time management, prioritising), emotional self‑regulation (big emotional reactions, quick frustration, rejection sensitivity), sleep, and motivation. ADHD often coexists with other conditions such as anxiety, depression, autism, learning difficulties, sleep disorders and substance use — these must be assessed and treated alongside ADHD when present. Diagnosis is clinical: it’s based on a history showing persistent, impairing patterns across multiple settings (home, school, work) that started in childhood, gathering collateral information (parents, teachers, past records), and ruling out other causes (medical issues, major mood disorders, situational stressors). There’s no single blood test or brain scan used in routine diagnosis. Effective treatment is multimodal and individualized: evidence supports stimulant medications (e.g., methylphenidate, dexamfetamine) as the most consistently effective for reducing core symptoms, with non‑stimulant medications as alternatives; psychological and behavioural approaches (psychoeducation, cognitive behavioural therapy adapted for ADHD, skills coaching, organisational and time‑management strategies), workplace or school adjustments, and supports for coexisting problems. Many people benefit most from combining medication (when appropriate) with behavioural strategies, coaching or therapy. The goal of care is to reduce impairment, improve daily functioning and quality of life, and teach practical strategies that work for the person’s routine and environment. ADHD is not a moral failing or simply laziness — it’s a diagnosable brain‑based condition. With accurate diagnosis and tailored supports, people with ADHD can manage symptoms and leverage their strengths (creativity, divergent thinking, hyperfocus on interests) to succeed in many areas of life.
